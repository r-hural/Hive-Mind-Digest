### 6. Making health habitual: the psychology of ‘habit-formation’ and general practice

bjgp.org · [open](https://bjgp.org/content/bjgp/62/605/664.full.pdf)

This British Journal of General Practice article (Gardner, Lally and Wardle, December 2012) argues that brief clinical advice should focus on how to build habits rather than only what to change and why. Habits are defined as actions triggered automatically by contextual cues that have been associated with their performance. The authors contend that repeating a simple action in a consistent context builds automaticity through associative learning, shifting initiation from conscious motivation to external cues, so habits can persist after interest fades. They argue that conventional behaviour-change advice, which engages deliberative processes and emphasises variety, tends to be short-lived and effortful. The article summarises supporting evidence: in a daily-repetition study, automaticity plateaued after an average of 66 days, missed occasions did not seriously set back progress, and simple actions became habitual faster than elaborate ones. A habit-based weight-control leaflet trial showed greater weight loss at 8 weeks (2 kg vs 0.4 kg) and an average 3.8 kg loss among completers at 32 weeks, with a primary care RCT underway. The authors also correct the 21-day habit myth. Practically, the article proposes a three-phase model (initiation, learning, stability) and a patient tick-sheet tool. Clinicians are advised to help patients pick a personally valued, small, new behaviour in a stable cue, expect roughly 10 weeks or more before automaticity, and avoid framing goals as giving things up. Caveats include variable habit-formation timelines, patient irritation at lifestyle advice, and the early nature of the evidence.
