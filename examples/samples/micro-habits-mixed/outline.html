1. What a habit is: automaticity and cue-driven behaviour

1.1 Defining habits: automatic responses to contextual cues

  • Within psychology, habits are defined as actions triggered automatically in response to contextual cues that have been associated with their performance. This differs from the everyday use of the word as a synonym for frequent or customary behaviour. [bjgp.org]
  • Habit-formation advice is to repeat a chosen behaviour in the same context until it becomes automatic and effortless. Advice on how to change, engaging automatic 'System 1' processes, may offer a valuable alternative with potential for long-term impact. [bjgp.org]
  • Varying behaviours and settings to maintain interest may stave off boredom, but it is effortful, depends on sustained motivation, and is incompatible with development of automaticity. [bjgp.org]

1.2 How repetition in a stable context builds automaticity

  • In a study where people repeated a self-chosen health behaviour after a once-daily cue, daily automaticity ratings rose asymptotically, with initial acceleration slowing to a plateau after an average of 66 days. [bjgp.org]
  • Simple actions reached peak automaticity more quickly than elaborate routines. Drinking water is an example of a simple action, and doing 50 sit-ups of a more elaborate one. [bjgp.org]
  • Among well-fitted participants, median time to 95% of asymptote was 91 days for exercise, 65 for eating and 59 for drinking. The subgroup comparison was not statistically significant (p = 0.328). [The Behavioral Scientist]
  • Verplanken (2006), cited in the Lally paper, showed that for the same number of repetitions a simple behaviour has a higher habit score than a complex behaviour. [The Behavioral Scientist]
  • Reported compliance correlated with model fit across the 39 well-fitted cases (r = 0.34, p = 0.035), though this does not isolate task complexity as the cause. [The Behavioral Scientist]

1.3 Measuring automaticity and the brain basis

  • Research attributed to MIT's Department of Brain and Cognitive Sciences is cited as showing that habits are formed in the basal ganglia, the part of the brain responsible for automatic behaviours. [Douglas Vandergraph]

2. Why motivation and willpower are unreliable for lasting change

2.1 Why motivation and deliberative advice fade

  • Advice on what to change and why engages slow, deliberative 'System 2' processes, and its effects are typically short-lived because motivation and attention wane. Even when patients successfully start the recommended changes, the gains are often transient because few traditional behaviour change strategies have built-in mechanisms for maintenance. [bjgp.org]

2.2 Routes to long-term change that bypass willpower

  • For long-term behaviour change there are only two ways to get it done. [TEDx Talks]
  • One should design for the behaviours that lead to an outcome rather than for the outcome itself, and most of those behaviours are habits. [TEDx Talks]

2.3 Evidence on how habits form and what they achieve

  • A new behaviour should be selected rather than giving up an existing one, because it is not possible to form a habit for not doing something; breaking existing habits requires different and more effortful strategies than making new habits. [bjgp.org]

2.4 Personal illustration of tiny habits

  • Starting with two push-ups after urinating, one practitioner progressed to five, then eight, and now often does about 12, adding up to roughly 50 to 70 push-ups a day depending on water intake and other factors. He believes the accumulated tiny habits have produced a long-term change, but says that will only be known in 5 or 10 years. [TEDx Talks]
  • Simply tweeting his weight for about a year in 2010 did not seem to have any effect on the speaker's weight. [TEDx Talks]

3. The behaviour model: motivation, ability and triggers

3.1 When a behaviour occurs: motivation, ability and a trigger

  • A behaviour occurs when motivation, ability and a trigger (a call to action) all happen at the same moment. If motivation and ability place the person above the curved line, the trigger will produce the behaviour. [TEDx Talks]
  • Motivation and ability trade off against each other along the curved line. [TEDx Talks]

4. Starting tiny: scaling behaviours down

4.1 Why shrink the behaviour

  • Relying primarily on motivation to change behaviour long-term is a losing strategy, and the same holds for willpower; making the behaviour change tiny enough takes motivation and willpower largely off the table. [TEDx Talks]
  • Advice on what to change and why engages slow, deliberative System 2 processes, and its effects are typically short-lived because motivation and attention wane. Brief advice on how to change, engaging automatic System 1 processes, may offer a valuable alternative with potential for long-term impact. [bjgp.org]
  • A habit must be established before it can be improved; it has to become the standard in life before it is optimised and scaled up. People get locked into optimising (the best workout program, ideal diet plan, perfect business idea) and so do not give themselves permission to show up in a small way. [AppSumo]
  • Aiming for small and manageable behaviour changes is encouraged because failure can be discouraging. [bjgp.org]

4.2 The two-minute rule

  • The two-minute rule says to take any habit you are trying to build and scale it down to something that takes two minutes or less to do. Examples: 'read 30 books a year' becomes 'read one page', and 'do yoga four days a week' becomes 'take out my yoga mat'. [AppSumo]
  • The two-minute rule helps people overcome the tendency to bite off more than they can chew and to master the art of showing up. [AppSumo]
  • A reader named Mitch, who ended up losing over a hundred pounds, limited himself to no more than five minutes at the gym for the first six weeks. The approach worked because he was mastering the art of showing up and becoming the type of person who went to the gym four days a week, even if only for two minutes. [AppSumo]
  • Starting with 5 to 10 minutes of movement rather than a daily gym session is suggested, on the grounds that small wins are more likely to trigger a dopamine response and create a positive feedback loop. [Douglas Vandergraph]

4.3 Evidence on small changes and habit formation

  • In one study, automaticity of a self-chosen health behaviour performed after a once-daily cue rose asymptotically and plateaued after an average of 66 days, with considerable variation across participants and behaviours. Missing the occasional opportunity did not seriously impair habit formation, as automaticity gains resumed after one missed performance. [bjgp.org]

5. Anchoring and cues: attaching new behaviours to existing routines

5.1 Using an existing behaviour as the trigger

  • Habits are actions triggered automatically in response to contextual cues that have been associated with them. Habit-formation advice is to repeat a chosen behaviour in the same context until it becomes automatic and effortless. [bjgp.org]

5.2 The 'After I..., I will...' format

  • The speaker has coached over 20,000 tiny habits using this approach. [TEDx Talks]

6. Celebration, small wins and positive feedback

6.1 Small wins and positive feedback loops

7. Showing up first, optimising later

8. How long habits take to form: from the 21-day myth to the 66-day estimate

8.1 Origin of the 21-day claim

  • Maxwell Maltz, in his 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics, wrote that it requires a minimum of about 21 days for an old mental image to dissolve and a new one to jell; the book sold more than 30 million copies. [James Clear, Douglas Vandergraph]
  • The '21 days to form a new habit' figure spread as people retold Maltz's story and shortened 'a minimum of about 21 days' into a flat 21-day claim, like a game of 'Telephone'; the same happened with 30 days or other magic numbers. [James Clear]
  • The 21-day belief appears to originate from anecdotal evidence of plastic surgery patients, who typically adjusted psychologically to their new appearance within 21 days. [bjgp.org]

8.2 The Lally study: 66 days on average, 18 to 254 days in range

  • In Lally's 12-week study of 96 people, each person chose one new behaviour and reported daily whether they did it and how automatic it felt; the average time for a new behaviour to become automatic was 66 days. Simple choices included drinking a bottle of water with lunch, and harder ones included running for 15 minutes before dinner. [James Clear]
  • The 254-day estimate exceeds the study length: the study ran 84 days (12 weeks), and the researchers used the data to estimate longer timelines. Estimates beyond 84 days projected the fitted curve past the period when reports were collected. [The Behavioral Scientist, James Clear]
  • Habits can take a long time to form, and the exact time depends on a variety of factors. [James Clear]

8.3 What the 66-day figure does and does not mean

  • The 66-day figure was the median estimated time to reach 95% of a modeled automaticity plateau for 39 participants whose data met the researchers' criteria. It was not a deadline for every person or behavior. [The Behavioral Scientist]
  • Of 96 participants, 82 provided enough data for analysis. Among them, 12 could not be fitted after 100 iterations, 8 produced flat lines, 16 had R² below 0.7, 4 had asymptotes below 21 out of 42, and 3 had asymptotes above 49. That left 39 participants, 48% of those with adequate data, with a 'good fit'. [The Behavioral Scientist]
  • A 2024 systematic review of health habit formation also found variation in the time taken and limitations in the available studies. [The Behavioral Scientist]

8.4 Realistic expectations and missed days

  • One suggested expectation is that building a new behaviour into life will probably take anywhere from two months to eight months, not 21 days. [James Clear]
  • Unrealistic expectations of how long habit formation takes can lead a patient to give up during the learning phase. [bjgp.org]

9. Limits and interpretation of the habit-timeline evidence

9.1 Who the 66-day figure describes

  • The selection criteria are not a validated test dividing people into permanent habit successes and failures. The researchers suggested that some participants whose curves could not be fitted, or whose estimated plateaus were very high, may simply have needed longer. [The Behavioral Scientist]

9.2 The 84-day window and extrapolated estimates

  • Among the 39 well-fitted participants, the median time to 95% of asymptote was 66 days, with a range of 18 to 254 days (roughly a fourteenfold difference). These were modeled times, and the study lasted 84 days, so any estimate beyond that point projected the fitted curve past the period when reports were collected. [The Behavioral Scientist, James Clear, Douglas Vandergraph]
  • Some modeled asymptotes (above 49 on a 42-point scale) were unrealistically high, meaning the model was extrapolating beyond the data. [The Behavioral Scientist]

9.3 Measurement of automaticity

  • The authors stated that they assessed the development of automaticity for performing the behaviours rather than specifically habit, and that new measures would be needed to disentangle the two forms of automaticity. Their measure could not establish whether a high score reflected habit or a flexible, goal-directed routine. [The Behavioral Scientist]
  • The study examined self-reported automaticity for chosen actions. Verplanken (2006), cited in the paper, had shown that for the same number of repetitions a simple behaviour has a higher habit score than a complex behaviour. [The Behavioral Scientist]

9.4 Behavior type and subgroup comparisons

  • The study does not establish that a whole exercise routine will become effortless. The exercise subgroup was small and its median modeled time exceeded the observation period. [The Behavioral Scientist]

9.5 What the figures do not show

  • A single missed opportunity did not materially disrupt reported automaticity: the measured change was less than half a point and scores recovered quickly. This is evidence about a single omission, not a test of repeated missed days or a long break. [The Behavioral Scientist, James Clear, bjgp.org]

  • The Lally study does not establish that a whole exercise routine will become effortless. It examined self-reported automaticity for chosen actions, the exercise subgroup was small, and its median modeled time exceeded the 84-day observation period. [The Behavioral Scientist]

12.3 Habit-based weight-control results

  • In a randomised trial, volunteers wanting to lose weight received a brief leaflet listing 10 simple diet and activity behaviours and encouraging context-dependent repetition. After 8 weeks the intervention group had lost 2 kg versus 0.4 kg in a no-treatment waiting-list control. At 32 weeks, completers in the intervention group had lost an average of 3.8 kg. [bjgp.org]
  • Interviews with participants in that trial indicated that automaticity had developed: behaviours became 'second nature', and participants 'felt quite strange' if they did not do them. Actions that were initially difficult became easier to maintain. [bjgp.org]
