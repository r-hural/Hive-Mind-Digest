# Micro-habits: small actions, lasting change, and evidence — Research Summary

## TL;DR
- A behaviour occurs when motivation, ability and a trigger combine at the same moment. Easy behaviours need little motivation and hard ones need a lot. Relying mainly on motivation or willpower is called a losing strategy, so the advice is to make behaviours tiny, anchor them to existing routines ("after I \[current habit\], I will \[new habit\]") and celebrate immediately.
- Scaling a habit down to a two-minute version (one page, taking out the yoga mat) is meant to establish showing up before optimising. Examples include a reader who limited early gym visits to five minutes and a medical student who studied 2 minutes a day. One author reports about a hundred books in 5 years, and bare-minimum days avoid a zero day.
- Specific plans improve follow-through: people who state a specific intention are two to three times more likely to follow through, and chronological when/where/how planning makes people nearly two times more likely to act. In a cited exercise study, about one in three tracking-only controls worked out versus nine in 10 who wrote a specific plan. A failure premortem and trigger alarms are also recommended.
- Habit-formation timing is contested. The 21-day claim, popularised by Maxwell Maltz in 1960, is described as not evidence-based. A review of more than 2600 participants found formation starts within about 2 months but ranges from 4 days to nearly a year (up to 335 days). One speaker's model gives about 66 days to automaticity, and newer research is described as suggesting 18 to 254 days.
- Lapses and environment: missing a day or two reportedly does not significantly derail habits, and self-compassion beats self-criticism. Adopting one habit at a time is advised. Phone distance is reported to cut focus by 10 to 20% when the phone is in view, and consistent sleep and wake times are called the most evidence-based tip. The compounding claim for 1% daily gains is stated inconsistently (37 times versus 38%).
