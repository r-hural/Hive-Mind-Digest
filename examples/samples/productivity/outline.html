1. What Productivity Means and Why Output Is Not Effort

1.1 Productivity as goal-directed progress

  • Productivity is defined as stuff you do that gets you toward your goals, and those goals are your own, not anyone else's. [Justin Sung]
  • Being productive is not the same as being busy, doing more things, or having great time management; none of these is productive unless it helps you achieve the goals that are important to you. [Justin Sung]
  • Productivity is described as moving toward your goals as effectively as possible: not getting more done, but getting more of the right things done. [Ross Harkness, Dan Zakaria, Dan Martell]

1.2 Output is not a measure of effort, self-control or character

  • The amount a person produces does not show how hard it was to make themselves work, and nothing can be told about a person's self-control, grit or fortitude from the amount of work they got done. [vlogbrothers]
  • Motivation accounts for only about 20 to 30% of whether people take action. [Justin Sung]
  • Common productivity advice will not suit every person, and failing to follow a piece of it does not mean the person is doing it wrong. [Mark Manson]
  • A person who loves their job but has no productivity habits is said to go further and last longer than a person who hates their job but has all the tricks, 10 times out of 10. [Mark Manson]

1.3 Long hours and busyness do not equal more output

  • As weekly hours rise, productivity plateaus and then falls, with diminishing returns at about 50 hours; someone working 70 hours produces about as much output as someone working 55 hours. [Justin Sung]
  • For knowledge work, output roughly doubles with twice the effort for a few hours, levels off from around hour four or five, and is almost completely flat by hours 10 or 12. [Mark Manson]
  • Working through exhaustion can produce work that has to be redone, making extra late hours counterproductive or even anti-productive. [Mark Manson]
  • Setting a daily 1500-word goal led one author to write thousands of useless words within a couple of weeks, creating more editing work and less progress on the book. [Mark Manson]
  • One speaker reports going from working 12 hours a day with no business progress to under 4 hours a day and well over $100,000 in 2023. [Ross Harkness]

2. Prioritisation, Doing Less and Saying No

2.1 Choosing a Few High-Leverage Priorities

  • Success is described as a subtractive process: winning comes from doing less, and the things a person says no to are what make the remaining yeses the right ones. The core productivity question is framed as how to do less and achieve more. [Dan Martell, Dan Zakaria]
  • Warren Buffett reportedly told a pilot to write down his top 25 goals, circle the five most important, and avoid goals 6 through 25 at all costs. [Daniel Pink, How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]
  • Cutting from ten focus areas to two is said to raise output beyond a simple redistribution. The stated reasons are that each area carries hidden admin and mental costs, that focus on fewer things builds momentum and flow, and that the subconscious gets room to ponder. The speaker finds two the sweet spot (one is too boring, three underperforms) and recommends committing for 30 to 90 days. [Outperform | Marvin Payne]
  • Small tasks are individually insignificant but together quickly become very significant, so they should be identified and reduced or cut as far as realistically possible. [Outperform | Marvin Payne]

2.2 The Pareto Principle and Most-Important-Task Methods

  • The Pareto principle states that for many outcomes 80% of results come from 20% of the causes. The suggested way to apply it is to choose as the most important task the one that, if completed, would get you 80% of the way to your goal. [SpoonFedStudy]
  • An 'inverted pyramid' allocates 3 hours to the most important task, 2 hours to the second and 1 hour to the third, with the most important task started first thing in the day, ideally right after waking. [SpoonFedStudy]
  • A brutally simple system is to write tasks on a simple list, circle the most important one and do it before anything else. This is said to force prioritisation, train discipline and eliminate decision fatigue. The speaker attributes to Sam Altman the view that people who move things forward choose the most important thing and do it. [Bill Rice Strategy]
  • Starting with the hardest task is advised, and Kellogg School of Management research is cited as finding people get more done and feel more accomplished when they tackle the hardest thing first rather than knocking off many easy things. Another speaker compares easy-first work to eating dessert before a meal. [Daniel Pink, Dan Martell]
  • Highest-priority work is placed in the speaker's peak-energy hours, found by tracking energy for a week. Deep work blocks of 60 to 120 minutes are reserved solely for the highest-priority tasks. [Dan Zakaria, Ross Harkness]

2.3 Limiting the To-Do List

  • Suggested daily list sizes vary: no more than five items; three to five items (because at 10 to 15 the person loses track of what is important); and about six top tasks checked off one by one. [Daniel Pink, My Simple Productivity System, Dan Zakaria]
  • Unfinished tasks (open loops) are described as the biggest harm to productivity, draining mental energy like many open browser tabs slowing a computer. [Dan Zakaria]
  • Shallow admin tasks are batched into a one-hour time block and finished there rather than expanding into the day. Another approach collects approvals, decisions and information requests into a single daily block, since many take only 5-10 minutes. [Dan Zakaria, Grace Beverley]
  • Parkinson's law holds that a task expands to fill the time given, so shorter deadlines or short time blocks are used to create urgency. [Dan Zakaria, Ross Harkness, Dan Martell]

2.4 Cutting Commitments and Saying No

  • Every yes to one thing is a no to whatever else could occupy that same time. [Dan Zakaria]
  • One speaker decides on yes or no by asking whether an opportunity helps achieve his goals, distracts from them, whether he wants to do it, and how it affects his other commitments. [Ross Harkness]
  • Tasks that are not important but need doing are automated (software, AI or outsourcing), while important tasks are systemised starting with a standard operating procedure. [Ross Harkness]

3. Motivation, Starting and Overcoming Procrastination

3.1 Motivation and Action as a Cycle

  • Motivation is not only the cause of action but also its effect, so once a person begins, it usually becomes harder to stop than to continue. [Mark Manson]
  • Harvard research cited in one source found that the biggest day-to-day motivator on the job is making progress in meaningful work, not praise, pay, or pressure. [Daniel Pink, A productivity system so good you HAVE…]
  • A long-term habit study from USC in 2020 found repetition, especially early on, to be the biggest predictor of long-term habit success, ahead of motivation, willpower, or rewards. [Daniel Pink]

3.2 Starting Without Motivation: Minimum Viable Actions and the Two-Minute Rule

  • Procrastination happens when a task feels overwhelming; the minimal viable action is the smallest action that feels completely doable in the moment. [Mark Manson]
  • The 2-minute rule shrinks a task until there is no reasonable excuse not to start. The dread is said to lie in the activation energy needed to start, not in the task itself, and once past the 2-minute mark momentum carries the person into the rest of the work. [millie liao]
  • The no zero days rule requires at least one small thing toward a goal every day, without needing to complete a project daily (for example, writing one sentence, doing one push-up, or reading one page). [Christian Claus]
  • Under no zero days, the least one could do is defined first; for a YouTube channel this meant daily tasks such as researching a topic, setting up a camera, writing one line of a script, or making one edit. Doing a little each day is said to compound into a finished product, such as a book finished by writing one word a day. [Christian Claus]
  • Tracking the no zero days streak (for example on a printout or in a bullet journal) is said to help consistency, and a longer streak made the speaker more resistant to breaking it, such as for alcohol-free days. [Christian Claus]

3.3 Making Progress Visible and Tasks Manageable

  • Splitting a big task into individual tasks and crossing each off gives a motivation boost after every item, so a detailed to-do list is said to make the motivation cycle smoother. [easy, actually]
  • Daily to-do lists can be limited to no more than five items. [Daniel Pink]

3.4 Habits, Cues and Reducing the Need for Willpower

  • Doing work at the same time every day and placing it inside an existing routine is said to eventually make not working feel weird. [easy, actually]
  • Framing a behavior as evidence of who you are, rather than as a to-do item, is said to make you less likely to skip it. A goal itself does not say what to do on a given evening when you don't feel like doing anything. [millie liao]
  • Using scripted actions is reported to halve the gap between intention and behavior, making it about twice as likely that a person does what they set out to do. [Justin Sung]
  • A habit requires a very clear cue and a response, so most complex behaviors are not habits. Once a behavior becomes a habit it no longer uses the prefrontal cortex. [Justin Sung]
  • Willpower is described as limited and easily drained, so the proposed fix is to remove the need for it by making decisions in advance. Most of a week's productivity is said to be determined by decisions made days earlier. [millie liao, Justin Sung, simple, actually]
  • Building the cues and architecture that make action natural takes iterations over weeks, maybe months, of experimenting and reflecting. [Justin Sung]

3.5 Fatigue, Breaks and Environment That Affect Starting

  • Taking a walk, ideally outside, is presented as a way to address tiredness, confusion, boredom, anxiety and distractions at once. [easy, actually]
  • Scrolling short-form video is high-stimulation input that does not work as real rest, and attention residue makes a 5-minute scroll easily become 25 minutes. [millie liao]
  • Putting the phone on silent in another room or hiding it is presented as better than only turning off notifications. [easy, actually]
  • Working from a bed mixes environment cues with sleep cues, so a clean designated work area is recommended; the speaker reports a 0% success rate for homework on the bed. [millie liao]
  • Switching the work location, even just facing a different direction, is said to work very well and pretty much never fail. [easy, actually]

4. Habits, Consistency and Streaks

4.1 Repetition, Cues and Routines

  • Flow state is described as designed through repeatable triggers such as location, chair and routine rather than arriving at random. [Dan Martell]

4.2 Systems Over Willpower and Motivation

  • Relying on willpower and motivation is described as unsustainable; people do not rise to the level of their goals but fall to the level of their systems. [The Productivity System I Taught to…]
  • People fall to the level of their environmental design rather than rising to the level of their motivation. [simple, actually]
  • Productivity is described as a self-reinforcing cycle in which finishing work creates motivation for more work; starting for even one minute can set the cycle going, so one need not wait for motivation. [easy, actually]

4.3 Streaks and Gamified Tracking

  • One gamified tracker gives one point per productive block, a reward at five points, and a reset when a day is missed. [simple, actually]
  • A friend's workout streak of about 1,200 days in a row is given as an example of streak tracking, with a simple daily win such as 10 sales calls. [Dan Martell]

5. Goal Setting, Progress Monitoring and Review

5.1 Writing and Limiting Goals

  • The group that wrote down their goals was around 42% more likely to achieve them than the group that set goals without writing them down. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]
  • Writing goals down is presented as the first evidence-based strategy for achieving goals, and the format does not matter much. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]
  • Setting too many goals is described as a mistake that can still undermine goal achievement even when the other strategies are used. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]

5.2 Goal Framing: Mental Contrasting and Identity

  • In some studies, people who spend too long visualising having already achieved their goals are less likely to achieve them. Visualising the outcome together with the obstacles (mental contrasting) boosts goal attainment. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]
  • The WOOP method, developed by psychologist Gabriele Oettingen, stands for wish, outcome, obstacle and plan. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]
  • In a voting study, people asked whether they would be a voter were way more likely to vote than people asked whether they would vote. A quote from Tony Robbins is also cited: the strongest force in human personality is the need to stay consistent in how we define ourselves. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]

5.3 Reviewing and Monitoring Progress

  • A meta-analysis cited as covering about 138 studies and nearly 20,000 participants found that regularly monitoring progress is one simple strategy that improves goal attainment. People who spend a small amount of time regularly monitoring their goals are significantly more likely to achieve them. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]
  • Reviewing written goals or weekly priorities daily takes about 10 seconds at most, according to the speaker. [How To Actually Achieve Your Goals in…]

5.4 Daily Priorities and Planning the Week

  • Each day gets a priority that defines a win. One speaker gives each day a number one priority task that must be done for the day to count as a win. Another chooses three non-negotiables per day, the three things that would make the day okay if only they were done, and blocks them into the calendar before other tasks. [David Keeler, Grace Beverley]

5.5 Weekly Review Routines

  • A weekly review is recommended. One speaker suggests 20 or 30 minutes on a good day, done on Friday afternoons. Another holds a one-hour weekly meeting with himself to review the previous week, categorise inputs, and plan the week ahead, calling it one of the few things that has deeply changed his life. [David Keeler, Nick Houchin]
  • The weekly review can be made a non-negotiable calendar appointment with yourself to manage the system and flush the capture methods. A weekly cadence works for one speaker, but someone with a lot going on may find a daily appointment works better. [How to Design A Perfect Productivity…]
  • Capture inboxes are the most important place where things get stuck, because they are the first way things enter the system; left uncleared, tasks, appointments and notes rot. The weekly quick-capture step aims to leave nothing in the capture inbox that needs processing. [How to Design A Perfect Productivity…, David Keeler]
  • In the weekly calendar review, the previous and following weeks are checked to confirm that main appointments and priorities are scheduled, so the calendar reflects one's values. [David Keeler]
  • One speaker's Friday closing shift starts around 3:30 p.m. The Monday to-do list is written on Friday, which the speaker says removes the Sunday scaries. [Grace Beverley]

5.6 Periodic Review of the Whole System

  • A reasonable check-in to revisit the whole system is every 3 months. The speaker advises against going below 3 months because several months in a structured system are needed to know what works. [A productivity system so good you HAVE…]
  • Starting a new system every two weeks is not recommended, because it causes whiplash and prevents getting anything done. [A productivity system so good you HAVE…]
  • After a very impactful life event, planned or unplanned, a system is often in chaos. [A productivity system so good you HAVE…]
  • When a system feels boring, it is not a failure and only means the novelty dial should be turned up slightly. When it feels chaotic, the comfort dial should be turned back up. [A productivity system so good you HAVE…]
  • Hand-designed systems often confuse what is fun to build with what is fun to use. The drop in enthusiasm after the honeymoon period is explained by hedonic adaptation. Aesthetics sit on top of the system and can be changed without altering it, whereas an aesthetic-led planner switch can change the system's foundations and force relearning. [A productivity system so good you HAVE…]

6. Time, Energy and Biological Rhythms

6.1 Energy versus time as the managed resource

  • Conventional wisdom treats time management as the foundation of productivity, using goal deadlines and detailed day planning. One speaker argues time is finite and uncontrollable, whereas energy can be systematically increased and regularly renewed, which makes it the more controllable resource. [Rowena Tsai]
  • Productivity is said to depend not on how many hours are put in but on how much energy or focused attention is invested during those hours. Others put it as needing more energy rather than more time, or as productivity shifting from time management toward energy management as skill grows. [Rowena Tsai, simple, actually, Dan Zakaria]
  • Energy here means being present and grounded rather than caffeine-style alertness, and it helps focus because attention is less divided. [Rowena Tsai]
  • One speaker says energy-draining people (energy vampires) go on a not-to-do list, because interactions either give or take energy. [Dan Martell]

6.2 Auditing and tracking energy, then scheduling around it

  • An energy audit begins by identifying which activities give energy and which drain it, typically on a self-made graph of energy levels across the day. A habit scorecard, in which every activity from waking to sleeping is rated plus, minus or neutral, is another prompt. [Rowena Tsai]
  • One speaker suggests tracking when energy is highest and lowest for one week, then scheduling the hardest and biggest tasks in peak-energy hours. His own peak is 8:00 a.m. to 1:00 p.m. [Dan Zakaria, Nischa]
  • Flow matching means matching the time of day when cognitive peak occurs with the activity. [Justin Sung]
  • For one speaker, a dip after dinner around eight or nine o'clock is restored by meditating, journaling or reading. Committing to one self-care activity done well is better than many done half-heartedly. [Rowena Tsai]
  • In a demonstration, a man did push-ups to failure and reported his energy rising from low to 9 out of 10 in 46 seconds. [Dan Martell]

6.3 Chronotype and peak times

  • One source divides people into three chronotypes: about 14% larks, who peak about 30 minutes after waking; about 20% owls, who peak in the evening; and the rest, third birds, who peak around 3 hours after waking. [Nischa]
  • About one in five people are night owls, for whom deep work is best scheduled much later in the day or even at night. For the other four in five, prime time is usually first thing in the day. [Daniel Pink]

6.4 Circadian timing of daily routines

  • One speaker says his lowest body temperature occurs about 2 hours before his average wake-up time, and that his best work is likely 4 to 6 hours after the temperature minimum. [Andrew Huberman]
  • The speaker recommends a morning outdoor walk for light exposure, as even under cloud cover more light reaches the eyes than from a very bright indoor bulb. He also delays caffeine to 90 to 120 minutes after waking to avoid a late-morning or afternoon crash. [Andrew Huberman]
  • The speaker fasts until about 11:00 a.m. or noon. [Andrew Huberman]
  • Body temperature peaks late in the afternoon and then drops; a drop of 1 to 3 degrees is said to be vitally important for falling asleep easily. Hot baths, showers or saunas followed by getting out may accelerate the drop. [Andrew Huberman]
  • Bright or moderately bright light between 10:00 p.m. and 4:00 a.m. is said to be strongly disruptive to dopamine production and sleep. Getting outside for 10 to 30 minutes in the afternoon or evening is recommended. [Andrew Huberman]

6.5 Ultradian cycles, work blocks and breaks

  • The brain is said to cycle through roughly 90-minute ultradian cycles across day and night, shifting between higher and lower alertness. [Andrew Huberman]

6.6 Fatigue, hours worked and recovery

  • Prefrontal cortex fatigue makes people more reluctant to make decisions and increasingly lazy, and pushing through lowers work quality. It does not necessarily recover with sleep, and a chronic state usually arises from inadequate recovery. [Justin Sung]
  • Working 12 hours a day may be productive for a couple of weeks but leads to burnout, whereas an efficient 5 to 6 hours a day with resets can be sustained for years. [Dan Zakaria, Ross Harkness]
  • Decision fatigue means only a limited number of good decisions can be made before quality drops, so fixed systems for recurring decisions reduce it. [Dan Zakaria]
  • Sleep of 7 to 9 hours, daily movement, healthy eating, water and mental health care are recommended as the things that make good work possible. [Nischa]
  • A shut-off routine of no work after 6:00 p.m. is described as a classic from Deep Work. [Dan Zakaria]

7. Deep Work, Focus Blocks and Time Constraints

7.1 Time blocking and fixed schedules

  • One speaker is a time blocker rather than a list maker: he is not a big believer in to-do lists and prefers to work with the actual available time, assigning each free block a job. [Huberman Lab Clips]
  • The same speaker calls his approach fixed schedule productivity: he fixes his work hours, works downstream from them for everything else, and has done so since grad school. [Huberman Lab Clips]
  • Deep work time should be blocked on the calendar the way meetings are blocked. [Future Simplified]
  • One speaker plans the week on Sunday and writes each day's tasks the night before in priority order. He then assigns each task to an hour, scheduling the highest-priority tasks first. [Ross Harkness]
  • Assigning each weekday a fixed theme (study on Monday, writing on Tuesday, editing on Wednesday, chill or catch-up on Friday) removes decision fatigue because the day's purpose is already known. [simple, actually]
  • Minute-by-minute time blocking worked for one creator only while his days were predictable (lectures, home, work, sleep). It deteriorated completely once college ended and holidays began. He still says time blocking is generally useful and needs a few subtle tweaks to be more realistic. [Odysseas]
  • The same creator found that packing the calendar with many time blocks for many interests caused paralysis of decision, and he ended up doing less. Leaving 10 or 20 minutes of unscheduled space between activities avoids the robotic feel of switching instantly, which had led him to burnout. [Odysseas]
  • The suggested alternative is to live weeks normally first and then design the ideal week from that, adding only one or two extra things such as language learning or boxing so the schedule stays rooted in reality. [Odysseas]

7.2 Protected deep work sessions

  • Recommended deep work block lengths are 60 to 120 minutes reserved for the highest-priority tasks, or a 90-minute block: choose one task, set a 90-minute timer, wear headphones with a do-not-disturb sign, do nothing else, then walk, hydrate and repeat. [Ross Harkness, simple, actually]
  • Deep work is described as by far the most productive way to work because it facilitates flow and focus. [Ross Harkness]
  • Deep work blocks come first thing in the morning, before email or social media. The hardest task comes first rather than the easiest. [Dan Martell, Daniel Pink]
  • Another speaker starts the day with deep work five days a week even in the busiest teaching semester, with more deep work on non-teaching days. In summer there are no meetings on Mondays and Fridays, admin is scheduled midday to early afternoon Tuesday through Thursday, and everything else is deep work. [Huberman Lab Clips]
  • Before working, one speaker puts his phone across the room, closes unneeded laptop tabs and shuts his door, which takes 2 minutes. [Ross Harkness]
  • Notifications can be switched off so that only a spouse and an assistant get through. One speaker reports that after setting up iPhone focus modes, screen time fell by well over half and notifications by about 80%. [Dan Martell, Future Simplified]
  • It takes 23 minutes on average to refocus after a distraction, and multitasking is described as doing multiple things poorly at once. Focus and distraction cannot coexist. [Dan Martell, simple, actually, Dan Zakaria]

7.3 Parkinson's law and deadlines

  • Parkinson's law is stated as work expanding to fill the time allocated to it: 3 days allotted means 3 days taken, and 3 weeks allotted means 3 weeks taken. [Dan Martell, Nischa, Dan Zakaria, Ross Harkness]
  • Shorter deadlines are suggested, such as 3 PM instead of end of week. [Dan Martell]
  • One creator found that video quality, views and comments barely changed whether a video took two days or two weeks, and that giving 90 minutes instead of a full day produced a quality not that much different. [Nischa]
  • Without real downside for not acting, most people do not act. [Dan Martell]

7.4 Working-hour limits and sustainable pacing

  • One speaker usually stops work at 5:30 p.m. Another describes a shut-off routine of not working after 6:00 p.m., which he calls a classic from Cal Newport's Deep Work. [Huberman Lab Clips, Dan Zakaria]
  • A 45 to 50 minute garage-gym workout before dinner marks the transition from work to family time. [Huberman Lab Clips]
  • Because insomnia could stop him working on any given day, one speaker defines productivity on a longer timescale: it does not matter if he works on the book tomorrow, but this month he must spend a lot of time on it. He also thinks about productivity at the scale of decades. [Huberman Lab Clips]
  • Time is set aside daily for doing nothing (walking, meditating, journaling) rather than scrolling or watching Netflix. Sleep of 7 to 9 hours is recommended. [Ross Harkness, Nischa]

8. Distraction, Phones and Environment Design

8.1 Measuring and cutting phone use

  • One creator counted 128 phone unlocks (pickups) in a single day, most of them brief checks during gaps rather than deliberate decisions to use the phone. The authors of Ultralearning recommend getting the daily pickup number below 90. [Nischa]
  • Another creator averaged 7 hours 24 minutes of screen time on the phone alone before a challenge in which distractions were removed. Stoic simplicity, meaning putting the laptop and phone away, was ranked A tier because the creator expected it to help a lot. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN)]
  • Blocking social media with a tap-to-block device was ranked S tier because it eliminates an insane amount of distractions. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack]
  • Staring at a wall works not because of the wall but because the person is not scrolling on the phone or checking email. The creator ranked the wall method A tier after the second session. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack]
  • Unfollowing or muting accounts that do not contribute to learning goals makes the social media feed work for the user rather than the reverse. [Dan Martell]

8.2 Notifications, phone setup and app blocking

  • One speaker turns off all notifications, allowing only his wife and his assistant to get through, on the view that people should be easy to find but hard to reach. [Dan Martell]
  • Rarely needed apps can be removed from the home screen instead of deleted, which declutters the phone while keeping them retrievable. [Future Simplified]
  • One speaker keeps the phone limited to productivity apps, almost never uses social media apps on it, and keeps banking and investment apps off it. He says the first part of a productivity system is carrying the system without the distracting apps that about 90% of people carry. [Liam Porritt]
  • The Endel focus app blocks other apps for, say, 60 minutes during a focus session, and the speaker works solidly for that time. [Liam Porritt]
  • Removing distracting apps and switching to black-and-white screen mode is said to work because people fall to the level of their environmental design rather than rising to the level of their motivation. [simple, actually]
  • Taking no phone for the first 30 minutes of the day is recommended, together with water, 5 minutes of movement and writing three things to get done. Deep work blocks are likewise placed first thing in the morning, before email or social media. [simple, actually, Dan Martell]
  • After three weeks of a grayscale screen plus a phone bedtime and lie-in, one creator reported feeling more refreshed and weirdly more rested, with nothing else changed. [Nischa]

8.3 Designing the workspace and surroundings

  • In an experiment by Princeton psychologist Sabine Kastner, a second distractor object in view left a faint trace in brain scans even when participants were told to focus fully on the first object. [millie liao]

9. Rest, Recovery, Sleep and Health as Foundations

9.1 Breaks and Downtime as Inputs to Output

  • Elite violinists studied by Anders Ericsson in 1993 practised in intense sessions, often 90 minutes long, and then took many breaks; rest was a key part of their peak performance. [Daniel Pink, simple, actually]
  • Restorative breaks are described by four principles: moving beats stationary, outside beats inside, social beats solo (even for introverts), and fully detached beats semi-detached, for example leaving the phone behind. [Daniel Pink]
  • There are said to be no rules on when to take breaks, only on how to take them: a person who cannot concentrate should stop pushing through and take a break, whether that is once an hour or twice every 10 minutes. [SpoonFedStudy]
  • Mental recovery means disconnecting from stimulation, for example by walking, meditating, journaling or just sitting, rather than scrolling a phone, watching Netflix or playing video games. One speaker schedules time each day for doing nothing. [Ross Harkness]
  • After three weeks of a phone bedtime and a lie-in plus grayscale, one creator reports feeling more refreshed and weirdly more rested, with nothing else changed. [Nischa]

9.2 Sleep and Circadian Timing

  • Two speakers prioritise 7 to 9 hours of sleep: one lists it with daily movement, healthy eating, water and mental health as what makes good work possible, and the other reports getting 7 to 9 hours of high-quality sleep each night. [Nischa, Ross Harkness]
  • A UK Biobank study of over 400,000 people linked too little daylight during the day directly to worse mood, worse sleep and disrupted circadian rhythms. [millie liao]
  • Even under cloud cover more light reaches the eyes than from a very bright indoor bulb, so a 10-minute outdoor walk is recommended. Getting outside in the afternoon or evening for 10 to 30 minutes is also recommended to lower late-evening retinal sensitivity to light. [Andrew Huberman]
  • Taking 300 to 400 mg of magnesium bisglycinate or threonate 30 to 60 minutes before sleep may help many people fall asleep. Taking 50 mg of apigenin 30 minutes before sleep may reduce rumination and anxiety. [Andrew Huberman]
  • The speaker reports that serotonin or its precursors taken in the evening disrupt sleep architecture for many people, including himself. [Andrew Huberman]

9.3 Exercise, Movement and Physical Health

  • A study in the Journal of Physiology is cited as showing that just 4 minutes of high-intensity exercise can quintuple production of BDNF. [SpoonFedStudy]
  • Hard workouts longer than an hour can be detrimental because they raise cortisol. About 80% of resistance training should stop short of failure, with about 20% taken to failure. [Andrew Huberman]
  • Brief walks of 5 to 30 minutes after eating can accelerate metabolism and improve nutrient utilization. [Andrew Huberman]
  • One speaker lifts weights and eats at least 1 gram of protein per pound of body weight daily while avoiding junk food and sugar. Another does a 45 to 50 minute garage-gym workout after work and before dinner, which marks the move from work to family time. [Ross Harkness, Huberman Lab Clips]
  • Outdoor forward movement such as walking produces optic flow, said to reduce neural activity in the amygdala. [Andrew Huberman]
  • At least 1,000 mg per day of the EPA form of omega-3 is said to be as effective as prescription antidepressants in relieving depression. [Andrew Huberman]

10. Capture, Organise and Review Systems

10.1 Capture: getting information out of the head

  • The brain is described as being for having ideas rather than storing them, so capture methods exist to move ideas out of the head into a place that can be returned to later. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…, How to Design A Perfect Productivity…]
  • Another speaker stopped trusting her brain to remember everything, calling brains terrible filing cabinets, and keeps a Notion inbox page pinned to her phone to capture thoughts immediately. [Laurie Wang]
  • A good productivity management system is described as having four basic parts: a capture method, a calendar, a task manager and somewhere to take notes. Another version names four tools: a notebook, a calendar, a to-do list and a way to store and organise files. [How to Design A Perfect Productivity…, My Simple Productivity System]
  • Information at work falls into four types: tasks, ideas, notes and media (digital files). Information originating from oneself is categorised as thoughts, while information from external sources such as meetings or YouTube videos is categorised as notes. [The Productivity System I Taught to…]
  • Quick capture works best in a tool built for speed. One speaker uses Google Keep because it is designed for quick capture rather than long-term storage. Another uses the free Todoist because its home-screen widget lets him input an item in under 10 seconds, and found a Notion template's loading animation and property fields added friction that reduced his motivation to write things down. [The Productivity System I Taught to…, My Simple Productivity System (for…]
  • Voice memos are called an underrated capture device for long, stream-of-consciousness brain dumps. [David Keeler]
  • Quick-capture entries should start with an action verb to avoid ambiguity (for example 'buy present' rather than 'present'). Every task is likewise phrased as a concrete action, an approach attributed to David Allen's Getting Things Done. [My Simple Productivity System (for…, My Simple Productivity System]
  • Ideally there is only one capture inbox, though sometimes multiple make sense. Another speaker advises as many capture methods as needed but as few as possible, because each one has to be cleared regularly. [David Keeler, How to Design A Perfect Productivity…]
  • The tool matters less than the system. The platform-agnostic core workflow is said to work with Notion, Todoist or Apple Notes. Another speaker says the choice of Notion, Evernote or Apple Notes matters less than having an organising system that does not slow you down. [The Productivity System I Taught to…, My Simple Productivity System]

10.2 Organise: sorting and routing what was captured

  • Per Thiago Forte, information should be organised by the location where it will be used, not by where it was found. The same speaker prefers a laptop for this step because he types faster on a keyboard and desktop navigation is easier. [My Simple Productivity System (for…]
  • One speaker sorts her captured notes once a day, usually in the morning before deep work, into categories (professional, personal digital admin, physical admin, calls and orders). During sorting, scattered thoughts become concrete next actions, such as a school trip becoming 'signing permission slips by Wednesday'. She says it takes ten minutes a day. [Laurie Wang]
  • One speaker puts tasks in Notion or Todoist and ideas and media in Obsidian. Another uses Apple Reminders with a catch-all list that feeds separate lists for jobs, university and YouTube projects. [David Keeler, Coffee and Casebooks]
  • Having more than one task manager or calendar is said to lead to confusion. Multiple task managers hold the same tasks in different formats, so the brain perceives multiple open loops and records often become inaccurate. [How to Design A Perfect Productivity…]
  • A common to-do list mistake, in one coach's experience, is not covering 100% of tasks. Tasks kept outside the list create low-level anxiety about forgetting them. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…, Nick Houchin]
  • The one-touch-to-inbox-zero approach attributed to Thiago Forte routes each email once: to the calendar, task manager, then archives it. Another speaker's version deals immediately with anything under two minutes and defers longer items by marking them unread. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…, Nick Houchin]
  • Gmail filters can reduce inbox clutter. One speaker skips emails containing the word 'unsubscribe' past the inbox, reasoning a real person is unlikely to write that word. Another sends them to archive or trash, routes a few essential newsletters to a folder, and auto-deletes those after 30 days. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…, Nick Houchin]
  • For an email requiring action more than a week away, one speaker snoozes it in Gmail until the Monday of the week it is needed. [My Simple Productivity System (for…]
  • Notes no longer needed can be moved to an archive folder so they stay searchable without cluttering active folders. Checklists reused more than once are kept separate from to-do lists. [My Simple Productivity System]
  • Storage and notes tools: one speaker keeps his whole life in Google Drive so every file survives loss or upgrade of a computer, while another stores large video files on hard drives because of their size. A third uses the Apple filing system, which syncs across phone, computer and iPad, with three top-level folders (personal, university, YouTube), and recommends scanning paper notebooks into it. Handwritten notes transferred into a digital notes app can be searched later. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…, My Simple Productivity System, Coffee and Casebooks, How to Design A Perfect Productivity…]
  • Read-later tools: one speaker uses the free Instapaper to separate reading from the moment of discovery. Another says a Read Later list can be kept in an ordinary notes app, so paid tools such as Instapaper, Matter or Readwise are not required. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…, Nick Houchin]
  • One speaker tried the Second Brain system but stopped because, at the time, the cost of maintaining it outweighed its benefit for his personal life. Another has used it since mid 2019 (about three years at recording). [Nick Houchin, How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…]

10.3 Scheduling: calendar and task planning

  • The calendar is called the most important tool in a basic system; the brain should not keep track of the schedule. A digital calendar is strongly recommended because schedules change constantly, and calendar items are entered the moment they arise. [Nick Houchin]
  • One speaker runs his life from his calendar, so anything not in it does not exist for him. A scheduling link from an app such as Calendly saves the back-and-forth of arranging meetings. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…]
  • The calendar shows non-negotiable immovable tasks and the baseline of available time; one speaker says you cannot be productive before seeing how much time you have. [Coffee and Casebooks]
  • Tasks should be given a time or place: if something does not have one it probably will not get done, and tasks in one demonstrated app are not properly sorted until given a date and time. One speaker blocks 45 minutes on Wednesday mornings to batch-fill content. [Laurie Wang, How to Design A Perfect Productivity…]
  • The calendar need not hold everything. One speaker time blocks and holds important appointments there rather than the full to-do list. Another says tasks that are not time-specific can stay in the task manager and be checked off when done. A third keeps his calendar as empty and clean as possible, colour-coded with multiple sub-calendars. [David Keeler, My Simple Productivity System (for…, My Simple Productivity System]
  • One speaker tracked every minute of his day in his calendar for three months and found it distracted him from work and did not make him happier or more productive. [My Simple Productivity System]
  • Calendar reminders (such as 'Start Book 7') can keep a reading goal of two books a month on track. [My Simple Productivity System]
  • Daily task load: one speaker keeps daily tasks to three to five items because at 10 to 15 he loses track of what is important. Another asks each day for the single most important task (a daily highlight) with a separate 'might do' list, and another gives each day one number-one priority task needed for the day to be a win. Unfinished tasks are moved to the next day and finished ones checked off. [My Simple Productivity System, How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…, David Keeler]
  • Task breakdown depends on familiarity: for a task done many times, a YouTube video breaks into write, film, edit and upload, but someone who has never made one may need 10 or 15 tasks. [My Simple Productivity System]
  • Analog to-do lists can work for a single day's tasks, but a digital task system for the larger system travels on the device so tasks can be captured the moment they appear. [Nick Houchin]

10.4 Review: keeping the system trusted

  • One speaker schedules three 30-minute review blocks every day: morning, after lunch and before signing off. Another checks inboxes at three set times (morning, before lunch, end of the workday) and keeps notifications off 90 to 95% of work time unless expecting an important call. [The Productivity System I Taught to…, Nick Houchin]
  • The rationale for scheduled reviews is that willpower and motivation are unsustainable: we do not rise to the level of our goals, we fall to the level of our systems (James Clear). [The Productivity System I Taught to…]
  • A decent system is said to cure burnout because one trusts the system instead of running low-level anxiety. The stated main purpose of a system is to do meaningful things efficiently, with less stress. [How to Build Your Ultimate Productivity…]
  • Advice on adopting a system: start small, set up systems and folder structures in advance and adapt as life changes; keep what works and is not getting in the way. One speaker found bigger systems did not fit life as a working parent, and the stripped-down system is the only one she has stuck to for over a year. Another recommends keeping tools as simple as possible, trusting one system. [My Simple Productivity System, Laurie Wang, Coffee and Casebooks]

11. Tools, Analog Versus Digital and Tool Minimalism

11.1 Paper versus digital: where each works best

  • A hybrid setup is described as the ideal: some things work better digitally and others on paper, so a combination that uses the strengths of both is the perfect productivity system. [Carl Pullein]
  • Initial planning is done on paper with the phone off and no screens, typically 30 to 40 minutes and sometimes an hour. Afterwards the key parts and next steps are scanned into a digital note so they are reachable on phone, laptop and iPad, and all output (writing, video, podcasts) is digital. [Carl Pullein]
  • Digital calendars beat paper calendars because appointments can be shared and added automatically to other people's calendars. A shared calendar with a partner lets each know the other's commitments. [Carl Pullein, Liam Porritt]
  • Digital to-do lists suit recurring and longer-term tasks, but a full on-screen list is distracting day to day. One approach is to copy the two or three must-do tasks onto a paper notebook kept flat on the desk, which takes about 50 seconds each morning. [Carl Pullein]
  • Weekly planning on paper takes 10 to 15 minutes, compared with about 30 to 40 minutes of screen-based digital planning previously. [Carl Pullein]
  • A digital journal in Day One was used for three solid years without the entries ever being reread, so the speaker switched to a paper journal in 2024. Another speaker journals in Day One every few days when he remembers, and considers that better than nothing. [Carl Pullein, My Complete Productivity System]
  • Paper is argued to be more durable than digital files. Steve Jobs's mid-1990s digital journal entries could reportedly not be printed about 20 years later, while about 7,000 pages of Leonardo da Vinci's paper notes from about 500 years ago remain readable. [Carl Pullein]
  • Paper is said to prompt more thinking than screens because screens show the same font and format in dark or light mode, whereas paper allows varied colours, formats and layouts. [Carl Pullein]

11.2 Notebook-first and handwritten approaches

  • One system uses just two tools, a paper notebook and the phone. Thinking happens on paper because the phone, even after being made less prone to doom scrolling, can still pull the user somewhere unplanned. [Nik Sol]
  • In an evening review of the day's notebook page, only what is worth keeping moves to digital tools (a books list in Notion with title and author, movies saved in a Taste app, a video idea as a duplicated script template with a working title). Most entries stay in the notebook, and an idea that feels stupid the next day is not transferred. [Nik Sol]
  • The notebook-first setup is said to reduce phone time and avoid overloading digital tools. [Nik Sol]
  • A spiral notebook is preferred over fancier notebooks because pages can be ripped out frequently and it lies flat when open. The uniball micro point 0.5 is named the best pen overall, and one notebook is used up every two or three weeks. [David Perell]
  • Writing is described as a tool for thinking, and the speaker would switch to a better method for thinking clearly if one existed but has not found one. The same speaker dictates by voice and has ChatGPT clean up the transcript, being more generative speaking than typing. [David Perell]

11.3 Checklists

  • In eight hospitals worldwide, a 19-item, two-minute surgical checklist was followed by complication rates falling 35% and death rates falling 47%. [This Productivity System Will Save Your…]
  • A checklist relieves the mind of having to remember, since the mind is a bad place to store things needed constantly. Checklists also include pause points, moments where a problem can be caught before anything can be done about it (before the first incision, or right before hitting record). [This Productivity System Will Save Your…]
  • To build a checklist, record and write out how you currently do a process, repeat it using the written version, refine it until it matches what you actually do, and only then teach it to others. [This Productivity System Will Save Your…]
  • The Apollo moon landing relied on a long checklist, with the crew simplifying the whole process to doing the next item on the list. A filmmaker with over 10 years of experience still sometimes films in slow motion or overexposes footage unintentionally, which a pre-shoot checklist could help with. Checklists are probably not going to solve all problems in life. [This Productivity System Will Save Your…]

11.4 Choosing note and task apps

  • Three things a note system must get right are structure, jot ability (one tap to start writing) and ease of use, mainly on mobile. Unstructured notes were never revisited: about one and a half thousand notes in Apple Notes were never looked at again. [Liam Porritt]
  • One speaker moved from Notion and ClickUp to Craft for personal notes because he wanted to tap once and start typing. He sees Craft as a middle ground between Apple Notes and Notion, with templates that Apple Notes lacks. [Liam Porritt]
  • Another speaker dabbled with Notion, Roam, Evernote, Obsidian and others and found simple note-taking is better for him. He uses Apple Notes for most note-taking, including first drafts of book chapters, because it feels less high-pressure than Notion or Google Docs. [My Complete Productivity System]
  • A kanban board (Trello) is the only task management style that works for one speaker. Any task system needs prioritization, checklist micro-tasks, automations and templates, and speed of capture. [Liam Porritt]
  • Todoist is described as simple, very cheap and cross-platform, with a usable free version for those not needing it with a team (as of 2022-03-25). Notion runs all projects across a team of 18. [My Complete Productivity System]
  • Google Tasks connects directly with Gmail and Google Calendar so tasks live where life already is, with zero friction. The speaker says he actually uses it, unlike 90% of fancy apps. [Dr. Matt Jones]
  • A paid email tool, Superhuman, is considered worth every penny by one speaker for people with a lot of email. Another speaker, as of 2022-03-25, puts it at $30 a month. [Dr. Matt Jones, My Complete Productivity System]
  • A screenshot of a handwritten or typed schedule can be turned into calendar events by Gemini, including on its free version (as of 2025-05-20). [Dr. Matt Jones]

11.5 Tool minimalism and against over-complex stacks

  • The more complicated a productivity system, the more time is wasted maintaining it, and more decisions mean more exhaustion and less real work done. Most productivity hacks are called disguised procrastination. [Bill Rice Strategy, SpoonFedStudy]
  • Productivity is said to have become a full-time job, with overbuilt systems such as Notion dashboards with 42 tabs taking more time to manage than the tasks themselves. The speaker claims all of his tools take less than 10 minutes a day. [Dr. Matt Jones]
  • A system that forces rigid conformity does not work for one speaker, so apps are used when needed, not religiously, and they get out of the way otherwise. [My Complete Productivity System]
  • The stated philosophy is elimination, not accumulation: reducing low-value items and focusing on what is genuinely important. [Carl Pullein]
  • Movies live in one dedicated app, with no system inside another system. The notebook is kept as a rough draft so Notion does not become a dumping ground. [Nik Sol]
  • Acting quickly even when messy is preferred over perfect planning, because speed compounds and momentum is more powerful than perfection. [Dr. Matt Jones]

11.6 Phone setup and focus tools

  • Writing the next day's plan and time-blocking it the night before is found more powerful than other planning methods. If something is not on the calendar it effectively does not exist, removing the mental strain of wondering what is next. [Liam Porritt, My Complete Productivity System]
  • A habit-tracking app is used for a single purpose: three workouts per week, running or walking twice a week, and no more than two takeaways per week. [Liam Porritt]

12. Enjoyment, Experiments and Planning Realism

12.1 Making Work Enjoyable

  • Productivity is framed as being less about apps, a perfect system, or being more disciplined or motivated than others, and more about having fun and learning joy in the journey; when people enjoy what they are doing, productivity takes care of itself. [5 Essential Tips for Long-Lasting…]
  • The 'work equals suffering' approach is rejected in favour of a balanced life. The 'find your passion' advice is said to fail for many people who do not know their passion, whereas learning to enjoy the things you are already doing does not depend on privilege or external circumstances. [5 Essential Tips for Long-Lasting…]
  • Approaching things sincerely rather than seriously (a phrase attributed to Alan Watts) helps make work feel like a game. Colour-coding revision subjects from red to yellow to green made studying feel more like a game in second-year medical school. [5 Essential Tips for Long-Lasting…]
  • Studying with music reduces focus very slightly according to the evidence, but it was kept because it makes work more fun. [5 Essential Tips for Long-Lasting…]
  • If a job feels ultimately meaningless, there is only so much hacking that can make it enjoyable, and the lack of meaning leaves a person unfulfilled. [5 Essential Tips for Long-Lasting…]
  • Editing video is described as more relaxing than watching a TV show, and the opportunity to work on things that matter has mattered most to this speaker's productivity. Kanban boards are used to spend less time on disliked parts of the work, possibly making something annoying more fun and rewarding. [vlogbrothers]
  • Reading a book was placed in B tier because the creator honestly enjoyed it, and no caffeine plus no music was placed in F tier as miserable. Ancient calisthenics earned S tier partly because the speaker enjoyed it. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack, I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN)]

12.2 Social and Competitive Motivation

  • Splitting a review of 2,000 patient records five ways among friends, with shared names on the resulting paper, got the work done in one evening. [5 Essential Tips for Long-Lasting…]
  • Competition was ranked A tier and chosen as the best ancient productivity hack, because the speaker's best performances came when with or competing against someone else. In a head-to-head round the speaker scored 19 rounds against the opponent's 21, losing but beating the previous day's result, and a 5K was run in 25:37 against a 26-minute goal. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN)]
  • External motivation can come from money: one speaker is motivated by it without needing it to reach their own bank account, which makes raising money for Partners in Health rewarding. [vlogbrothers]

12.3 Testing Hacks

  • The creator ran a challenge adding three new productivity hacks per day over a few days and ranked them in a tier list. Cleaning the room was ranked S tier and named the number one hack, as the first step without which the others would not have been done. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack]
  • Hacks that removed distractions ranked highly: blocking social media with a tap-to-block device went to S tier, and the wall method went to A tier. The wall trick is said to work because the person is not doing something else, such as scrolling or checking email. Stoic simplicity (putting laptop and phone away) was A tier; the speaker had averaged 7 hours 24 minutes of phone screen time before the challenge. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack, I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN)]
  • The Pomodoro timer went to A tier. The rationale given is that it is easier to convince yourself to focus for 25 minutes than for a whole afternoon. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack]
  • In the ancient-hack challenge, the candle method got S tier and the to-do list A tier. Meditation reached A tier after a best session on the final day, with S tier possible with another week or two of practice, though the first 20-minute attempt had been unfocused. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN)]
  • Several hacks fared poorly: mouth tape (F tier, with an unrefreshing sleep), a cold shower (immediate F tier), pink noise (D tier), fasting (F tier), the Egyptian wooden pillow (F tier), and walking everywhere (D tier). [I Tested Every Productivity Hack, I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN)]
  • Sleeping on the floor was placed in B tier after the speaker woke with a bad headache, though unsure whether the floor caused it. The 5:00 a.m. wake-up was also B tier, disliked but known to be good for the creator. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN), I Tested Every Productivity Hack]
  • Running was placed in C tier as good for you but with other productive things possible. Fitness and rock climbing were A tier, with a suspicion that something else might be a better use of time. [I Tested Every Productivity Hack (AGAIN), I Tested Every Productivity Hack]

12.4 Planning Realism and Flexibility

  • Life can be enjoyed now as much as later, and promising yourself a future happiness that may never arrive is described as a dangerous game. [Odysseas]
  • Daily work is sorted into quick ticks (under 5 minutes), tasks (5 to 30 minutes) and projects (30 minutes plus). Three non-negotiables are chosen each day, being the three things that would make the day okay if only they got done. [Grace Beverley]
  • Recurring decisions are automated: investing and savings go out by standing order on payday, razors and skin care refills are on subscription, and a regular food shop is a saved template reordered in about 40 seconds. Systems are said to matter even more than discipline, with no 5:00 a.m. wake-up, cold plunge or 3-hour morning routine required. [Grace Beverley, Dan Zakaria]
  • Goals are split across six life categories (health, career and business, relationships, personal development, finances, mindset) to avoid over-indexing on work. [Grace Beverley]
